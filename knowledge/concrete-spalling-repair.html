Concrete Spalling Repair: A Practical Guide

By Geraldo Monte · 8 August 2026 · Concrete repair & remediation

Spalling — concrete breaking away from a structure, usually exposing corroded reinforcement — is the most common concrete defect in New Zealand buildings, bridges and infrastructure. It is also the most commonly repaired badly. This guide covers what causes spalling, how to assess it properly, and how to repair it so the repair lasts.

What causes spalling

By far the most common cause is corrosion of the reinforcing steel. Steel needs two things to corrode: oxygen and moisture, both of which reach the bar through the concrete. Once the protective alkaline environment of the concrete is lost (through carbonation — CO₂ neutralising the concrete from the surface inward) or overwhelmed by chlorides (from salt, marine air, or de-icing), the bar starts to rust.

Rust occupies roughly two to four times the volume of the steel it consumes. That expansion pushes outward on the surrounding concrete cover. Eventually the cover cannot take the pressure and breaks away — that's the spall. This is why spalling is a surface symptom of a hidden, ongoing process, and why the process must be addressed, not just the hole.

Other causes include:

  • Freeze-thaw cycles — water freezing in the pores of saturated concrete.
  • Alkali-silica reaction (ASR) — a chemical reaction between reactive aggregates and alkalis in the cement that expands over years.
  • Fire damage — high heat spalls concrete explosively or progressively, especially in the surface layer.
  • Mechanical damage — impact, overloading, or poor detailing concentrating stress.

Assess before you touch anything

A good repair starts with a good diagnosis. Rushing to chip and patch — without understanding the extent or the cause — is how repairs fail within a few years.

If the element is structural — a beam, column, slab or bridge member — the repair strategy should be confirmed by a structural engineer before work starts. They define how much bar section loss is tolerable, whether temporary propping is needed, and what the repaired element must achieve.

The patch repair process, step by step

For localised spalling with limited corrosion, the process is:

  1. Break out to sound concrete. Remove all delaminated, carbonated or chloride-contaminated concrete around the bars. Sawcut clean, square edges at the repair perimeter (don't feather edges), and break out to a minimum depth — commonly at least 10–20 mm behind the reinforcing bars so the repair mortar fully surrounds the steel.
  2. Clean and assess the reinforcement. Grit blast or needle-gun the exposed bars to remove rust. Check remaining section. If section loss exceeds the engineer's threshold (commonly around 20–25%), the bar is supplemented or replaced.
  3. Protect or treat the steel. Depending on the specification: a passivating/rebar primer, a cementitious coating, or — where corrosion is severe — simply ensuring the bar is fully embedded in chloride-resistant repair mortar. Some specifications use sacrificial or impressed-current cathodic protection for widespread cases.
  4. Prepare the substrate. The prepared concrete must be sound, clean, and free of laitance and dust. Immediately before applying the repair material, bring the substrate to a saturated-surface-dry (SSD) state — damp, but with no standing water. Dry substrate will suck water out of the repair mortar and kill the bond.
  5. Apply the repair material. Use an appropriate repair mortar or concrete — hand-applied polymer-modified cementitious mortar (PCC) for patches, or sprayed concrete (shotcrete) for larger areas. Apply in layers per the manufacturer's limits, and compact well into the edges and behind the bars to avoid voids.
  6. Cure the repair. Repair mortars are more sensitive to early drying than structural concrete. Follow the manufacturer's curing requirement exactly — typically wet curing or covering for 24–48 hours minimum, sometimes longer.
  7. Protect the repaired element. Where corrosion was chloride-driven or widespread, a protective coating or membrane over the repaired area — and often the whole element — extends the repair life significantly.

When a patch is not enough

Patch repair has a well-documented weakness: it fixes the visible damage but can leave the surrounding, apparently-sound concrete still corroding — the incipient anode effect, where corrosion activity shifts to the concrete next to the new patch. For localised, shallow damage this is manageable. For widespread chloride contamination, heavy section loss, or prestressed elements, honest options include:

  • Full element replacement — the durable answer where the structure is beyond economic repair.
  • Concrete jackets / encasement — adding new concrete around the element to restore section and protect the steel.
  • Cathodic protection — actively stopping corrosion over large areas, the long-term answer for marine structures.
  • Waterproofing / coating systems — cutting off moisture and chlorides at the surface, applied as a preventive strategy across whole elements or structures.

A good repair contractor will tell you when patching is the wrong answer — even if it costs them the job. Repairing concrete is not about making the spall disappear; it is about stopping the process that caused it.

Standards and references

This guide is written in the author's own words; for the authoritative text, consult the standards and guides below.

  • NZS 3101.1:2006 — Concrete structures standard (design and construction), the New Zealand backbone for concrete durability and repair interfaces. Available from Standards New Zealand.
  • EN 1504 series — Products and systems for the protection and repair of concrete structures; EN 1504-3 (structural repair products) and EN 1504-10 (application of products) are the most relevant parts. Commonly specified in NZ repair specifications.
  • ACI 546R-96 — Concrete Repair Guide; the classic international reference on repair methodology, diagnosis and materials selection. Available from concrete.org (ACI).
  • ACI 562-19 — Code Requirements for Assessment, Repair and Rehabilitation of Existing Concrete Structures. Available from concrete.org (ACI).
  • Concrete NZ — Industry guidance on concrete durability, delamination and repair (formerly CCANZ). Available from concretenz.org.nz.
  • BRANZ — New Zealand building research guidance, including repair of concrete in buildings. Available from branz.co.nz.

Frequently asked questions

What causes concrete spalling?

The most common cause is corrosion of the reinforcing steel: rust expands to several times the volume of the steel it consumes, and the expansion pushes the concrete cover off. Other causes include freeze-thaw cycles, alkali-silica reaction, fire damage, and mechanical impact or overloading.

Can you just fill spalled concrete with mortar?

No. Filling the hole without removing the cause — usually corroding reinforcement — guarantees the repair fails again, often faster than the original concrete did. Durable repair requires breaking out to sound concrete, treating the steel, and using a suitable repair material applied properly.

How long does a concrete patch repair last?

A well-executed patch repair on concrete with limited active corrosion can last decades. But patch repair does not stop ongoing corrosion elsewhere; where chloride contamination or corrosion is widespread, patching alone has a poor record and options like full element replacement, jackets or cathodic protection should be considered.

When is spalling a structural problem?

When significant reinforcing bar section is lost (commonly flagged at roughly 20–25% section loss), when prestressing strands are involved, or when the remaining concrete section cannot carry the design loads. Any suspected structural spalling should be assessed by a structural engineer before repair.

What standards apply to concrete repair in New Zealand?

NZS 3101 covers concrete structures design and construction in New Zealand. For repair products and systems, the EN 1504 series is the internationally recognised framework (often referenced in NZ specifications), and Concrete NZ and BRANZ publish local practice guidance for assessment and repair.

The bottom line

Spalling is a symptom, not the disease. Diagnose the cause, treat the steel, prepare the substrate, use the right material, cure it properly — and be honest about when patching is no longer the right answer. Done that way, a repair is an investment. Done the fast way, it is a recurring cost.
