Concrete Curing: Why It Matters and How to Do It Right

By Geraldo Monte · 8 August 2026 · Concrete construction

Curing is the most underrated step in concrete construction. It is also the one most often skipped, rushed, or done badly — because by the time the failure shows up, the forms are long gone and the crew has moved to the next job. But curing decides whether the surface of your concrete is hard, dense and durable, or dusty, cracked and permeable.

What curing actually does

Concrete gains strength through hydration — a chemical reaction between cement and water that continues for weeks and months. The reaction needs two things: water and a reasonable temperature. Curing is simply the practice of keeping those conditions in place long enough for the concrete to develop the strength and durability it was designed for.

The catch is that the surface of fresh concrete loses water to evaporation far faster than the interior. If the surface dries out in the first days, the reaction stops at the surface layer — the exact layer that has to resist wear, weather, carbonation and chlorides. The result is a slab that passes its cylinder tests but has a weak, permeable skin.

What happens when curing is done badly

  • Plastic shrinkage cracking — fine, shallow cracks from rapid surface drying, often appearing within hours of placement.
  • Dusting and weak surfaces — a soft, powdery wearing surface that never hardens properly.
  • Higher permeability — water, chlorides and CO₂ penetrate faster, which accelerates corrosion of reinforcing steel and reduces the service life of the structure.
  • Crazing — a network of fine map-like cracks on the surface.
  • Early stripping damage — removing forms before the concrete has enough strength, causing edge and corner damage.

The frustrating part is that none of these show up on a 28-day compressive strength report for the core concrete. The failure is in the surface layer — which is why bad curing is so often invisible until it costs someone money.

The standard curing methods

The right method depends on geometry, access, weather and whether the surface needs to bond to something later (a curing compound can interfere with subsequent coatings or toppings — check compatibility before choosing it).

How long to cure

Standard practice under NZS 3101 / AS 3600-type regimes is a minimum of 7 days of continuous moisture retention for ordinary Portland cement concrete at moderate temperatures. Three days can be enough for high-early-strength concrete that has reached adequate strength. For durability-critical elements — bridge decks, marine structures, exposed flatwork — longer curing (14 days or more) is routinely specified and is cheap insurance.

Duration should be measured from placement, not from when someone gets around to it. Interrupted curing (wet for two days, dry for two, wet again) is far less effective than continuous curing.

Temperature matters

  • Hot weather: above roughly 30°C ambient, or in wind and low humidity, evaporation is aggressive. Start curing as early as the surface can accept it, use fogging or early covering, and shade if practical. Placement temperatures above about 35°C are commonly restricted in specifications.
  • Cold weather: below about 5°C, hydration slows dramatically. If fresh concrete freezes, water in the mix expands and can permanently damage the structure. Cold-weather work needs protection, insulation and sometimes heating until the concrete reaches a safe strength — and the curing period is typically extended.

Practical rules that protect the pour

  1. Plan curing before the pour — have covers, water, or compound on site and a person assigned to start it.
  2. Start as early as the surface can take it without marking — within a few hours of finishing for flatwork in normal conditions.
  3. Keep it continuous. Interrupted curing is where the failures live.
  4. Don't strip forms early just to keep the programme moving — formwork retention is free curing.
  5. For repairs and toppings, follow the manufacturer's curing requirement to the day — repair mortars are often more sensitive to early drying than structural concrete.

Frequently asked questions

How long should concrete be cured?

A minimum of 7 days of continuous moisture retention is standard practice for ordinary Portland cement concrete at moderate temperatures. Longer curing (14–28 days) improves durability, and hot, windy or dry conditions require more aggressive curing, not less.

Can you cure concrete with plastic sheeting?

Yes. Plastic sheeting is an effective curing method as long as it is laid as soon as the surface can take it without marking, fully sealed at the edges and joints, and held in close contact with the surface so condensed moisture stays on the concrete.

What happens if you don't cure concrete?

Uncured concrete loses surface moisture too fast. You get plastic shrinkage cracking, weaker and dustier surfaces, higher permeability and lower long-term durability — even if the 28-day strength test still passes, the surface layer is compromised.

How soon can you walk on newly placed concrete?

Walking is usually possible once the surface has set hard enough not to mark — commonly 24 to 48 hours depending on mix and temperature. Full design loads should not be applied until the specified strength is reached, typically at or beyond 7 days.

Does concrete cure in cold weather?

Yes, but much more slowly. Below about 5°C hydration slows markedly, and if fresh concrete freezes, permanent strength and durability loss can occur. Cold-weather concreting needs protection and often heating until the concrete reaches a safe strength.

The bottom line

Curing is not an optional extra — it is part of the specification. The cheapest, most reliable durability upgrade available to a construction team is to cure concrete properly for seven days. The next time a programme is tight and someone suggests skipping the cure "to get ahead", remember: you are not saving time, you are borrowing it from the service life of the structure — with interest.
